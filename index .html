What does the market have to believe about SoFi?

A residual income valuation of SoFi Technologies, cross-checked against two peer sets, plus a reverse-engineering of the assumptions already embedded in the share price.

Why residual income rather than DCF. Free cash flow is the wrong lens for a bank. Deposits are not financing; they are raw material, the input the business converts into loans. The usual separation of operating and financing cash flow therefore collapses. Capital is also regulated rather than discretionary: growth in lending forces growth in equity. Residual income sidesteps both by starting from reported tangible book value and valuing only the returns earned above the 14.0% cost of equity.

Method. Three-year projections built from the 2025 annual report and the first half of 2026. Cost of equity from CAPM (4.69% risk-free rate, 2.20 beta, 4.23% equity risk premium). Valuation by residual income and justified P/TBV, benchmarked against both a traditional bank comp set and a fintech comp set, with a sensitivity grid flexing terminal ROTCE against cost of equity.

Three of four methods cluster at $10 to $13 because they treat SoFi as a bank. The fintech comp set says $20.94 because it treats SoFi as a growth platform. The market cannot decide which company this is, and that indecision is worth roughly 2× on the share price.

What I'd flag before anyone else does. The fintech peer set is bimodal: a small number of expensive names pull the median upward, so $20.94 is closer to an artifact than a signal. The 2.20 beta reflects how SoFi trades rather than what its balance sheet holds; the sensitivity grid shows what happens at lower figures. Terminal ROTCE is the assumption everything downstream hangs on.

Do fintechs generate different complaints than banks?

Comparing Chime, Block, PayPal, and Coinbase against Wells Fargo across 30 months of federal complaint data. Not just how many complaints each attracts, but what they are about and how they end.

Approach. Roughly 30 months of CFPB Consumer Complaint Database records, cleaned in Python and analysed in SQL, published as a Power BI dashboard. The interesting comparison is not volume, since Wells Fargo dominates on raw count simply because it is larger. What matters is composition and outcome: which issues dominate for each firm, and how often a complaint ends in monetary relief rather than an explanation.

Findings pending. The cleaning pipeline and analysis queries are written and published; the dashboard is in build.

Known limitation, stated up front. The CFPB publishes no customer-count denominator, and customer figures across these five firms are not defined consistently enough to construct one. Every comparison is therefore expressed as a share within company rather than a rate per customer.
